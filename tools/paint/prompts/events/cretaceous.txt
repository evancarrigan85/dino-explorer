A painting for a natural-history museum: The asteroid, about 66 million years ago.
Painted in the manner of Charles R. Knight (1874–1953), the American natural-history muralist: oil on canvas, confident visible brushwork, warm golden naturalistic light, soft atmospheric haze, a palette of ochres, siennas, olive greens and dusty blues, calm and dignified realism. Keep his warmth and his eye for the real world, but follow TODAY's science, not the science of his time: paint the land, water, plants and climate exactly as described here.
What happened, from the scientific evidence: A rock about 10 km wide is falling towards the shallow sea that covers the Yucatán. Impact. The blast is felt around the world; molten rock rains back down and sets forests alight. Soot and dust block the Sun. For months, perhaps two years, almost nothing can grow. Plants die, then the plant-eaters, then the hunters. The sky clears on a different world. Three in four species are gone — every dinosaur except the birds.
Paint the moment as seen from high in the sky: a wide view of the land and sea where it happened, as they were THEN — the continents, coasts, plants and life of that time, not today's. Show only what the description says. Living things only where it names them, small, as they are reconstructed today; none where it does not.

A scientific reviewer turned down an earlier painting of this. Do NOT repeat any of these mistakes: Shows a falling rock, but the massive impact explosion is already occurring simultaneously below it. Geography resembles modern Central America, not the Late Cretaceous. Temporal paradox of simultaneous falling and exploding, plus modern geography. The geography shows a modern Isthmus of Panama and Mexico, which did not exist in this form 66 million years ago. Temporal error: The asteroid is still falling in the sky while the impact crater and plume are already fully formed. The geography shows modern Central America, not the Late Cretaceous layout, and the rock is shown intact while hitting. The map shows modern geography, including the Isthmus of Panama. There is a signature in the bottom right corner. The geography is anachronistic and the impact physics are inaccurate. The perspective is confused, mixing a continental satellite view with visible, ground-level dinosaurs. An asteroid hitting at cosmic speeds would vaporize in a blinding flash, not remain an intact rock plowing into the earth.
