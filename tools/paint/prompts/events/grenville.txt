A painting for a natural-history museum: The Grenville mountains, about 1050 million years ago.
Painted in the manner of Charles R. Knight (1874–1953), the American natural-history muralist: oil on canvas, confident visible brushwork, warm golden naturalistic light, soft atmospheric haze, a palette of ochres, siennas, olive greens and dusty blues, calm and dignified realism. Keep his warmth and his eye for the real world, but follow TODAY's science, not the science of his time: paint the land, water, plants and climate exactly as described here.
What happened, from the scientific evidence: Continents collide and raise a long mountain range along eastern North America. Worn down, its roots are the old rocks of the Adirondacks and of southern Ontario, Quebec and Labrador.
Paint the moment as seen from high in the sky: a wide view of the land and sea where it happened, as they were THEN — the continents, coasts, plants and life of that time, not today's. Show only what the description says. Living things only where it names them, small, as they are reconstructed today; none where it does not.
What could not yet exist then, so must not be painted: NO plants of any kind on land, nothing green or leafy (dark crusts of microbes on damp ground are fine): all life is in the water; NO animals anywhere except, at most, simple soft-bodied sea creatures. The land is real, weathered ground, not a stage: grey and red rock, gravel, and wide sheets of sand and mud spread by rain and broad shallow rivers (with no roots to hold the soil, rivers ran in wide braided sheets), wind-rippled sand, dark wrinkled crusts of microbial mats on damp ground and tidal flats, and a sea full of life.
A scientific reviewer turned down an earlier painting of this. Do NOT repeat any of these mistakes: Tentacled creatures in water are too advanced. Signature text is visible.
