A painting for a natural-history museum: The Capitanian crisis, about 260 million years ago.
Painted in the manner of Charles R. Knight (1874–1953), the American natural-history muralist: oil on canvas, confident visible brushwork, warm golden naturalistic light, soft atmospheric haze, a palette of ochres, siennas, olive greens and dusty blues, calm and dignified realism. Keep his warmth and his eye for the real world, but follow TODAY's science, not the science of his time: paint the land, water, plants and climate exactly as described here.
What happened, from the scientific evidence: Lava floods across what is now south-west China — the Emeishan eruptions — and life suffers a mass extinction in the sea and on land. The dinocephalians vanish.
Paint the moment as seen from high in the sky: a wide view of the land and sea where it happened, as they were THEN — the continents, coasts, plants and life of that time, not today's. Show only what the description says. Living things only where it names them, small, as they are reconstructed today; none where it does not.
What could not yet exist then, so must not be painted: NO palm trees (palms appear about 100 million years ago) and nothing palm-shaped (no tall slender trunk with a crown of big fan or feather leaves), and NO flowering plants or flowers. The trees are conifers (pine-like and monkey-puzzle-like), seed ferns like Glossopteris with tongue-shaped leaves, tree ferns, and cycads — cycads only as SHORT, stout, barrel-trunked plants no taller than a person. Where it is wet the ground is covered with ferns and horsetails (there is no grass), in thick stands; where it is dry, scattered low shrubs and bare ground. Plants grow as they do in nature: in continuous stands, thickets and carpets where there is water, thinning gradually where it is drier — never single plants dotted evenly over bare ground like a model.
