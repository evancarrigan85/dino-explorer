A painting for a natural-history museum: When the Earth froze over, about 722 million years ago.
Painted in the manner of Charles R. Knight (1874–1953), the American natural-history muralist: oil on canvas, confident visible brushwork, warm golden naturalistic light, soft atmospheric haze, a palette of ochres, siennas, olive greens and dusty blues, calm and dignified realism. Keep his warmth and his eye for the real world, but follow TODAY's science, not the science of his time: paint the land, water, plants and climate exactly as described here.
What happened, from the scientific evidence: Twice, ice spread from the poles to the tropics and stayed for millions of years — then melted fast. 720 million years ago. The supercontinent Rodinia is breaking up, and many of its pieces lie near the equator. 717 million years ago, just as huge eruptions flood Arctic Canada with lava, the ice begins. White ice reflects sunlight back into space, so the cold feeds itself. Ice spreads from the poles toward the equator. The world stays frozen for nearly 60 million years: the Sturtian glaciation. Whether the oceans froze over completely, or kept a strip of open water near the equator, is argued over. Volcanoes keep breathing out carbon dioxide. With the land frozen, rain can no longer wash it out of the air, so it builds up — until the ice melts. Less than 20 million years later the world freezes again: the Marinoan glaciation. 635 million years ago comes the great thaw. The ice melts quickly, and thick layers of limestone — “cap carbonates” — settle on top of the rocks the glaciers left. Life came through, perhaps in pools of meltwater on the ice. Soon afterwards, the first large living things appear.
Paint the moment as seen from high in the sky: a wide view of the land and sea where it happened, as they were THEN — the continents, coasts, plants and life of that time, not today's. Show only what the description says. Living things only where it names them, small, as they are reconstructed today; none where it does not.
No text, labels, map lines, arrows, borders or frame.
