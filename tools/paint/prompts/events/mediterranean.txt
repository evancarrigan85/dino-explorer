A painting for a natural-history museum: When the Mediterranean dried up, about 6.2 million years ago.
Painted in the manner of Charles R. Knight (1874–1953), the American natural-history muralist: oil on canvas, confident visible brushwork, warm golden naturalistic light, soft atmospheric haze, a palette of ochres, siennas, olive greens and dusty blues, calm and dignified realism. Keep his warmth and his eye for the real world, but follow TODAY's science, not the science of his time: paint the land, water, plants and climate exactly as described here.
What happened, from the scientific evidence: Cut off from the ocean, a whole sea turns to salt — then the Atlantic breaks back in. About 6 million years ago the Mediterranean is joined to the Atlantic by channels through what is now southern Spain and Morocco. As Africa pushes into Europe, those channels rise and narrow. Less ocean water can get in. The sun evaporates the sea faster than rivers can refill it. Salt starts to pile up on the sea floor. Over about 600,000 years, salt and gypsum build up in layers that are kilometres thick in places. At the lowest, much of the sea may have shrunk to salty lakes far below today’s sea level. 5.33 million years ago the Atlantic breaks through at Gibraltar. Water pours in — at its peak perhaps a thousand times the flow of the Amazon. The sea may have refilled in as little as a few months to two years. The salt is still there, buried under the sea floor. That is how it was found.
Paint the moment as seen from high in the sky: a wide view of the land and sea where it happened, as they were THEN — the continents, coasts, plants and life of that time, not today's. Show only what the description says. Living things only where it names them, small, as they are reconstructed today; none where it does not.
