A painting for a natural-history museum: How the Himalayas rose, about 80 million years ago.
Painted in the manner of Charles R. Knight (1874–1953), the American natural-history muralist: oil on canvas, confident visible brushwork, warm golden naturalistic light, soft atmospheric haze, a palette of ochres, siennas, olive greens and dusty blues, calm and dignified realism. Keep his warmth and his eye for the real world, but follow TODAY's science, not the science of his time: paint the land, water, plants and climate exactly as described here.
What happened, from the scientific evidence: India races north across an ocean, crashes into Asia, and pushes up the highest mountains on Earth. 80 million years ago India is an island continent far to the south, moving north across an ocean. It moves up to about 15 centimetres a year — four times faster than your fingernails grow, and very fast for a continent. Around 66 million years ago lava floods out across western India: the Deccan eruptions, at the time the dinosaurs died out. About 50 million years ago India meets Asia. The ocean floor between them has been used up, and the crust starts to crumple. India keeps pushing. The crust buckles and stacks up into the Himalayas and the high Tibetan Plateau. India is still moving north, about 4 to 5 centimetres a year, so the mountains are still being pushed up — even as rain and ice wear them down. Fossils of sea creatures are found near the top of Everest: its summit rock was once the floor of an ocean.
Paint the moment as seen from high in the sky: a wide view of the land and sea where it happened, as they were THEN — the continents, coasts, plants and life of that time, not today's. Show only what the description says. Living things only where it names them, small, as they are reconstructed today; none where it does not.
No text, labels, map lines, arrows, borders or frame.
