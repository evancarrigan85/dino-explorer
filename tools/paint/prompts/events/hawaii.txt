A painting for a natural-history museum: How Hawaii’s islands were born, about 5.6 million years ago.
Painted in the manner of Charles R. Knight (1874–1953), the American natural-history muralist: oil on canvas, confident visible brushwork, warm golden naturalistic light, soft atmospheric haze, a palette of ochres, siennas, olive greens and dusty blues, calm and dignified realism. Keep his warmth and his eye for the real world, but follow TODAY's science, not the science of his time: paint the land, water, plants and climate exactly as described here.
What happened, from the scientific evidence: A hot spot in the mantle builds volcano after volcano as the Pacific plate slides over it like a conveyor belt. Deep under the Pacific, a hot spot of rising rock melts its way up and builds volcanoes on the sea floor. About 5 million years ago Kauaʻi rises out of the sea over the hot spot. The Pacific plate slides north-west, about 7 to 9 centimetres a year, carrying each island away. Cut off from the hot spot, its volcano goes quiet. A new volcano grows over the hot spot, then another: Oʻahu, then Molokaʻi, Maui and the rest. Old islands wear down and slowly sink as they move away. That is why Kauaʻi is the most rugged and the Big Island the most volcanic. Half a million years ago the Big Island begins. Its volcanoes Kīlauea and Mauna Loa are still erupting today. Under the sea to its south-east, the next island is already growing: Kamaʻehuakanaloa. It may reach the surface in tens of thousands of years.
Paint the moment as seen from high in the sky: a wide view of the land and sea where it happened, as they were THEN — the continents, coasts, plants and life of that time, not today's. Show only what the description says. Living things only where it names them, small, as they are reconstructed today; none where it does not.
No text, labels, map lines, arrows, borders or frame.
