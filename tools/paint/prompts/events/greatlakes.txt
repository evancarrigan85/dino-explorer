A painting for a natural-history museum: How the Great Lakes were made, about 26,000 years ago.
Painted in the manner of Charles R. Knight (1874–1953), the American natural-history muralist: oil on canvas, confident visible brushwork, warm golden naturalistic light, soft atmospheric haze, a palette of ochres, siennas, olive greens and dusty blues, calm and dignified realism. Keep his warmth and his eye for the real world, but follow TODAY's science, not the science of his time: paint the land, water, plants and climate exactly as described here.
What happened, from the scientific evidence: An ice sheet three kilometres thick scrapes out five basins — then melts, and fills them. 26,000 years ago. Ice up to 3 km thick covers Canada and the northern United States. The ice is moving. Rocks frozen into its base scrape like sandpaper, deepening old river valleys and soft rock into huge basins. About 18,000 years ago the climate warms, and the ice edge starts to melt back north. Meltwater pools in the scooped-out basins in front of the ice — lakes bigger than today’s, spilling south down the Mississippi. About 12,500 years ago water starts pouring over a ridge of hard rock: Niagara Falls begins. It has cut a gorge 11 km long since. To the north-west, Lake Agassiz grows along the ice edge — at its largest, bigger than all of today’s Great Lakes together. About 8,200 years ago the ice holding it back gives way, and Lake Agassiz drains into Hudson Bay very quickly. The North Atlantic cools for a century or two. The Great Lakes settle into roughly today’s shape. Freed of the ice’s weight, the land is still rising — around Hudson Bay by about a centimetre a year.
Paint the moment as seen from high in the sky: a wide view of the land and sea where it happened, as they were THEN — the continents, coasts, plants and life of that time, not today's. Show only what the description says. Living things only where it names them, small, as they are reconstructed today; none where it does not.

A scientific reviewer turned down an earlier painting of this. Do NOT repeat any of these mistakes: Shows circular craters with raised rims, not the elongated, irregular basins of the Great Lakes. The basins are perfectly round craters, not glacially scoured river valleys. Water is pooling, but in geologically incorrect circular craters. The crater-like geology is incorrect for this event and location. A signature is visible in the bottom right corner. Fails to depict glacial scouring of valleys, showing impact-style craters instead. Basins resemble impact craters or volcanic calderas with raised rims, which is geologically inaccurate for glacial scouring. Foreground trees distort the scale, making the continental ice sheet appear like a small, local glacier.
