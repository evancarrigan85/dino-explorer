A painting for a natural-history museum: The Ediacaran creatures vanish, about 540 million years ago.
Painted in the manner of Charles R. Knight (1874–1953), the American natural-history muralist: oil on canvas, confident visible brushwork, warm golden naturalistic light, soft atmospheric haze, a palette of ochres, siennas, olive greens and dusty blues, calm and dignified realism. Keep his warmth and his eye for the real world, but follow TODAY's science, not the science of his time: paint the land, water, plants and climate exactly as described here.
What happened, from the scientific evidence: Most of the strange, quilted creatures of the Ediacaran disappear as the first burrowing, grazing animals spread. Whether it counts as a mass extinction is argued over.
Paint the moment as seen from high in the sky: a wide view of the land and sea where it happened, as they were THEN — the continents, coasts, plants and life of that time, not today's. Show only what the description says. Living things only where it names them, small, as they are reconstructed today; none where it does not.
What could not yet exist then, so must not be painted: NO plants of any kind on land, nothing green or leafy (dark crusts of microbes on damp ground are fine): all life is in the water. The land is real, weathered ground, not a stage: grey and red rock, gravel, and wide sheets of sand and mud spread by rain and broad shallow rivers (with no roots to hold the soil, rivers ran in wide braided sheets), wind-rippled sand, dark wrinkled crusts of microbial mats on damp ground and tidal flats, and a sea full of life.
A scientific reviewer turned down an earlier painting of this. Do NOT repeat any of these mistakes: Extensive green patches on land resemble plants. Green land cover violates the bare rock constraint. Scale is highly confusing; foreground creatures appear massive relative to the river delta.
No text of any kind: no signature, monogram, initials or date (not even small, in a corner), no labels, map lines, arrows, border or frame.
