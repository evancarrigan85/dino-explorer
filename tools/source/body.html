How this map is made

The land moves the way the science says it did. Every piece of today's coastline is attached to the tectonic plate it sits on and carried back to where that plate was — using a published reconstruction of plate motions covering the last billion years (Merdith and colleagues, 2021).

The outlines are today's coasts. That makes continents recognisable, but real shorelines changed as seas rose and fell — for instance, a seaway covered the middle of North America in the Cretaceous. Before 410 million years ago the map shows the model's ancient continental blocks instead.

Green, tan and white are a sketch, not a measurement: a simple picture of wet, dry and cold belts by latitude, with ice where the rocks record ice ages. Before plants reached land, the land is drawn bare.

Ice Age sea level. In the last 2.6 million years, ice sheets locked up so much water that the sea fell by up to about 125 m, turning shallow seafloor into dry land — the paler ground around today's coasts. The coast moves level by level (10, 20, 30… 125 m) using a world depth grid about 37 km per cell, so small islands and narrow straits are approximate. Sea level is the global average — the last 30,000 years follow published reconstructions closely; older ice ages are simplified. Locally the land itself rose or sank too, especially where heavy ice sheets had pressed it down.

Mountains. For the Ice Age and after, the darker ground is today's high country (above 600, 1,500 and 3,000 m). Mountain ranges are much younger than continents, so further back than about 5 million years they are not drawn — nobody should read the Himalayas into a world that had none.

Temperature. The 🌡 line and the coloured stripe under the timeline show Earth's average surface temperature, from a published reconstruction covering the last 540 million years (one value per million years). Inside the Ice Age the swings were much faster than that, so there the figure is scaled from sea level — about 6 °C colder than today at the coldest. Treat every value as “about”.

Empires and kingdoms. In the Humans tab, for the last 5,500 years, the coloured washes are rough hand sketches of each state's greatest reach, drawn from standard historical atlases. Real borders were fuzzy, shifting and often disputed, and every state was smaller before and after its height — read them as “about here”, not as surveyed lines. Sizes and peak dates follow published tables.

Fossil markers sit where each fossil was found, moved to where that spot was when the animal was alive.
