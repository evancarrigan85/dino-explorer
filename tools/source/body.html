How this map is made

The land moves the way the science says it did. Every piece of today's coastline is attached to the tectonic plate it sits on and carried back to where that plate was — using a published reconstruction of plate motions covering the last billion years (Merdith and colleagues, 2021). Before that the map follows Cao and colleagues (2024), who carried the same plates back to 1.8 billion years; between 1,050 and 1,000 million years ago, where the two disagree by up to 10°, it eases from one to the other. The further back, the less certain: before about 1,000 million years ago, where each piece sat is argued over, and other models put them elsewhere. Before 1.8 billion years ago no published model says where the land was, so the map draws none: it shows the sea alone and says so. The life of that time is listed without places on the map. Before about 4.4 billion years ago the sea is coloured red-hot, because the young Earth’s surface may have been molten; the oldest crystals say there was liquid water by 4.4 billion years ago.

Seas over the land. The sea has often spread far over the continents — in the Ordovician about two-fifths of their area lay under water. From 540 million years ago to the start of the Ice Age, the pale blue is sea over the continents and their drowned edges (down to about 1,400 m), and the land is drawn only where the coast then stood. Coast and edge come from published maps of the ancient Earth’s height and depth (Scotese and Wright, 2018; CC BY 4.0). The blue stripes are land those maps call dry but that the sea covered when it rose highest: published coast maps (Kocsis and Scotese, 2021; CC BY 4.0) push the shore out to every fossil of a sea creature of that age. Both were made with another plate model (PALEOMAP); here every point is turned back to today’s map with that model and then carried on our own plates, so near the edges of plates the two can disagree a little — and where the model we use joins its parts (about 100, 250 and 410 million years ago) a few pieces jump hundreds of kilometres at once, which the map slides over a million years. There is one map every 5 million years with points about 100 km apart, so small islands and narrow straits are approximate, and the coast changes from one map to the next instead of sliding. No such maps exist before 540 million years ago, so there the land is drawn whole.

Heights and depths. Over the same span, the light and shadow on the land and the blue of the shallow seas — palest where the water was shallowest, darker where it deepened — come from published maps of the ancient Earth’s height and depth (Scotese and Wright, 2018; CC BY 4.0): one map every 5 million years, a point about every 100 km, carried on our plates and blended smoothly from one map to the next. They are estimates. The height of a mountain range that wore away long ago is judged from its rocks and from mountains like it today, so read the relief as “high ground here”, not as a height in metres. The slopes are drawn about 90 times steeper than they are, or they would be too gentle to see. The browser’s graphics chip (WebGL) draws this, loading each map as you need it, so a copy of the page saved on its own, or a browser without WebGL, shows the map without it.

The dotted line is today’s coast, carried back on its plate, so you can see which of today’s lands lay under the sea. Before 410 million years ago the map shows the plate model’s ancient continental blocks instead of today’s coasts, and no dotted line. For the last 3 million years the coasts are today’s, with the Ice Age shorelines below.

Where the deep-time land has colour and grain, rocks put it there. Some rocks only form in one kind of climate: coal in swampy forest, salt and desert-soil crusts where it is dry, bauxite and kaolin where it is hot and wet, and the rubble dropped by glaciers where there was ice. About 8,500 dated sites of these, from a published atlas, are painted onto the land as soft patches a few hundred kilometres across, fading at their edges — green with trees, sandy, deep green, or white — each carried on its plate and shown for as long as the rock’s age allows, which is often ten or twenty million years. It is coarse, and it is evidence rather than a picture: land with no patch has no such rock in the atlas, not no climate.

Green, tan and white are a sketch, not a measurement: a simple picture of wet, dry and cold belts by latitude, with ice where the rocks record ice ages. Before plants reached land, the land is drawn bare.

Ice Age sea level. As the ice sheets grew they locked up so much water that the sea fell — by about 130 m at the coldest moments — turning shallow sea floor into dry land: the paler ground around today’s coasts. The level comes from published records: the last 30,000 years from dated ancient shorelines (Lambeck and others, 2014); back to 800,000 years from a stack of seven sea-level records read from deep-sea mud (Spratt and Lisiecki, 2016), good to about ±10 m; and back to 3.6 million years from an ice-sheet model run through the same mud (Berends and others, 2021). The stack smooths the peaks a little: dated corals show the sea stood several metres above today’s about 125,000 years ago (Dutton and Lambeck, 2012), where the stack shows about today’s level. The coast moves level by level (10, 20, 30… 125 m) on a world depth grid about 37 km per cell, so small islands and narrow straits are approximate, and seas higher than today’s are not drawn. Locally the land itself rose or sank too, especially where heavy ice sheets had pressed it down.

Ice sheets. For the last 80,000 years the ice is a published reconstruction drawn every 2,500 years (PaleoMIST, Gowan and others, 2021); the map fades from one to the next rather than sliding the edge, because nobody knows exactly where it stood in between. Before that it uses the outlines Batchelor and others (2019) drew for twelve moments back to 3.6 million years ago, mostly the coldest peaks. An outline is shown only while the sea level says the ice was near that size, so in the warm spells between, only Greenland’s ice remains. Some long stretches were never mapped — 1.8 to 0.9 million years ago among them — and there the nearest outline in time stands in, paler, with a finer dotted edge. Patagonia’s ice of 20,000 years ago stands in the same way for older southern ice ages.

Landscapes. For recorded history — about the last 6,000 years — the land is coloured and textured from measured climate: rainforest, savanna, dry grassland, desert, temperate and Mediterranean country, northern forest, tundra and ice, following a published map of the world’s climate zones (1986–2010) thinned to about 50 km. Further back the deserts and forests were in different places — the Sahara was green 8,000 years ago — so before then the land goes back to the simple sketch. Research can say what the land was like in deep time too, from coal, salt and glacial rocks; that layer is not built yet.

Close up. Zoom in on the last two million years and the land shows today’s ridges and valleys as light and shadow, from a world shaded-relief image about 4 km per pixel (Natural Earth, via the open basemap package). Zoom further in on the last few thousand years and the coastline switches to a detailed one, about 1 km. Both show today’s land, so they fade out further back, where the coasts and mountains were different. They load in pieces as you need them; a copy of this page saved on its own will not have them.

Rivers and lakes. Today’s great rivers (Natural Earth, public domain) are drawn back to 3 million years ago: most of them already ran roughly where they run now, and where the ice lay they are hidden under it. Not all: the Thames ran further north, through the Vale of St Albans, until the ice pushed it into its present valley about 450,000 years ago (Gibbard, 1977), and the map does not show that. Today’s lakes are drawn only after the last Ice Age, about 12,000 years ago. When the sea was low the rivers ran on across the dry sea floor. Those courses are traced, not mapped: from each river mouth, the way water would run downhill over today’s sea floor to deep water, with the ice as a wall. So when ice filled the northern North Sea, the Rhine meets the Seine in the Channel River, as the river’s mud off France records (Toucanne and others, 2009); when it did not, the Rhine runs north; and before the Strait of Dover was cut, about 450,000 years ago (Gupta and others, 2017), it cannot go south at all. The traces follow today’s sea floor on a 37 km grid, so they are routes, not channels — mud has since filled the real ones — and the lakes that formed against the ice are not drawn.

Mountains. For the Ice Age and after, the darker ground is today's high country (above 600, 1,500 and 3,000 m). Mountain ranges are much younger than continents, so further back than about 5 million years they are not drawn — nobody should read the Himalayas into a world that had none.

Temperature. The 🌡 line and the coloured stripe under the timeline show Earth's average surface temperature, from a published reconstruction covering the last 540 million years (one value per million years). Inside the Ice Age the swings were much faster than that, so there the figure is scaled from sea level — about 6 °C colder than today at the coldest. Treat every value as “about”.

Empires and kingdoms. In the Humans tab, for the last 5,500 years, the coloured washes are rough hand sketches of each state's greatest reach, drawn from standard historical atlases. Real borders were fuzzy, shifting and often disputed, and every state was smaller before and after its height — read them as “about here”, not as surveyed lines. Sizes and peak dates follow published tables.

Sounds of the time. The 🔈 Sound button plays a soundscape made in your browser, never a recording, and only of things there is evidence for. Wind and waves are always there, and the wind blows harder when the world is colder; in a Snowball Earth there is only wind, over ice. From 165 million years ago you hear insect song: a pure, low note of about 6.4 kHz in short pulses, rebuilt from the wing of the katydid Archaboilus musicus (Gu and others, 2012). Insects may well have sung before, but that is the oldest song anyone has rebuilt. From 67 million years ago there are bird calls, because Vegavis has the oldest fossil voice box (syrinx) yet found (Clarke and others, 2016); birds may have called earlier. The calls are simple chirps, not any real species.

Backdrops and moving water. The 🎨 button changes what the map sits on: an old atlas page; a field book, after Edward Lhwyd’s 1699 guide to fossils and the first woodcuts of them, with an engraver’s lined sea; a workshop table; a planetarium dome; or space. While you explore, light plays on the water, glints flash and clouds drift — westward in the tropics and eastward further out, the way the great wind belts blow today. They are decoration: they are not the weather of that day, and they stop if your device asks for less motion.

The real shape. On the globe, the Geology tab can show today’s gravity shape (the geoid): the level the sea would settle at if it could flow under the land, which rises and falls by up to about 100 m around the smooth shape the Earth is usually drawn as. It comes from EGM2008, a gravity model built from twin satellites, ships, planes and the height of the sea (Pavlis and others, 2012), averaged into 2° squares and smoothed so that only features wider than a few hundred kilometres show, and every bump is drawn 10,000 times taller so it can be seen. The bulge at the equator (21 km) is left out, because at that size it would squash the globe flat. It is shown for today only: there is no gravity map of the ancient Earth.

Fossil markers sit where each fossil was found, moved to where that spot was when the animal was alive.

How we check this map. Every change is tested against evidence the map was not built from.
